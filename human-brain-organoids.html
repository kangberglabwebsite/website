Human brain organoids & 3D models

We develop human organoids, cerebroids and assembloids to model brain development in three dimensions and to study interactions among neural stem cells, neurons, glia, microglia and vascular cells.

Building experimental models of the human brain

Human brain development depends on interactions among many cell types organised across space and time. Two-dimensional cultures are powerful for many questions, but they cannot fully reproduce the architecture and multicellular environment of developing tissue.

Our lab uses several complementary 3D systems, including stem cell-derived organoids, multicellular assembloids and ex vivo human fetal brain cerebroids. Each model offers different strengths, allowing us to choose the system that best matches the biological question.

  • Cortical and hippocampal organoids
  • Human fetal brain cerebroids
  • Assembloids incorporating microglia and vascular cell types
  • Models of the developing choroid plexus
  • Single-cell, imaging and functional analysis of 3D neural tissue

Cerebroids

Preserving native human tissue organisation

Our cerebroid platform uses small pieces of developing human brain tissue maintained ex vivo in three dimensions. This approach preserves native cellular diversity and aspects of tissue architecture while allowing controlled experimental perturbation.

We have used cerebroids to examine how inflammatory signals influence neural stem cells and cortical organisation. Because the system retains cell types that are absent from many conventional organoids, it can reveal tissue-level effects that would otherwise be missed.

More complete multicellular models

We are also interested in bringing additional cell types into 3D neural models. Microglia, endothelial cells and perivascular cells contribute to brain development, tissue remodelling and responses to inflammation, yet they are often missing from standard organoid systems.

By incorporating these components, we aim to build models that better represent the cellular interactions of the developing and ageing human brain and that can be used to test mechanisms of disease.
